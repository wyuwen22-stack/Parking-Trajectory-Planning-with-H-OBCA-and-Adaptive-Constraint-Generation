Diagnosing and Recovering Hybrid A* Failures in Narrow Parking

This study examines why a Hybrid A* front end fails on a subset of a 128-case parking benchmark, and evaluates a recovery strategy without relaxing vehicle geometry or obstacle clearance. The investigation identifies two distinct problems: the search can discard useful vehicle poses during state merging, and a valid search curve can lose clearance when converted into an interpolated output sequence. Goal-side search with finer heading bins recovered usable geometric paths in 22 of the 24 baseline failure cases after a separate export correction. Two cases remain unresolved.

1. Observed failures and investigation scope

The benchmark contains parallel, perpendicular and angled parking, irregular obstacles, and 20 adapted TPCAP cases. Its 128 cases represent 39 distinct obstacle layouts; several cases differ only in their initial pose. The vehicle is 4.7 m long and 2.0 m wide, with a 2.7 m wheelbase and a minimum turning radius of 3.95 m. The HA geometric checks require 0.05 m obstacle clearance. This requirement concerns the geometric initial path; it is separate from the acceptance rules for the downstream trajectory optimizer.

In the original run with a 90 s search limit, HA returned a path in 112/128 cases. Of those returned paths, eight failed the existing clearance check on the exported pose sequence. The resulting failure cohort therefore contains 24 cases: 16 with no returned path and eight with an unacceptable output representation. These outcomes should not be grouped under a single explanation such as insufficient computation time or physical infeasibility.

The parallel-parking failures provide a useful starting point for diagnosis. Nine of ten initial poses failed in the 6.0 m slot, whereas all twenty cases in the 6.5 m and 7.0 m slots returned paths. This concentration suggests sensitivity to the narrow feasible passage in vehicle configuration space. It does not, by itself, establish whether the cause is inadequate clearance, a restrictive motion model, or a search implementation that loses necessary states.

Earlier forward retries without a time limit recovered four TPCAP cases, bringing the historical clearance-accepted total to 108/128. They did not recover the nine narrow parallel-parking failures. All 24 cases from the original failure cohort were retained for the subsequent recovery experiment, including those four already recovered cases, so that the cohort definition did not change after observing results.

2. Diagnostic evidence and causal hypotheses

The diagnosis began by distinguishing a truncated search from a completed search over the states retained by the implementation. With no time limit, the nine narrow parallel cases exhausted their queues after approximately 80,800–82,700 expansions, below the 200,000-expansion cap. Once the queue is empty, a longer time allowance cannot generate additional candidates under the same deterministic search rules. This observation rules out a time cutoff as the direct stopping cause for these runs. It does not prove that the underlying continuous planning problem has no solution.

The next checks examined the geometry and the heuristic map. In the narrow parallel layout, the goal vehicle has 0.25 m obstacle clearance. A representative failed initial pose also clears the obstacles, and its two-dimensional distance heuristic is finite under the existing five-cell inflation. The inflated free-space map remains connected to the goal. These checks reject the specific explanation that inflation completely seals the slot entrance in this layout. They do not establish that the heuristic is otherwise irrelevant: expansion order can still determine which continuous pose survives within a discrete search bin.

Geometric feasibility was then tested separately from planner success. A known valid parking path was connected to the other initial poses through the open aisle using straight and curved segments. The resulting paths retained the original turning-radius and clearance requirements and passed interval body-clearance checks. These constructed paths served as feasibility witnesses, not as independent HA successes. Their value was diagnostic: for this layout and geometric model, the failed searches had missed feasible paths rather than demonstrated that the vehicle could not park.

Inspection of state merging exposed a concrete mechanism. The implementation uses 0.2 m position bins and 5° heading bins, retaining one lowest-path-cost pose per key. Each motion primitive is 0.3 m long, and the largest heading change in one primitive is 0.3 / 3.95 = 0.07595 rad, or approximately 4.35°. A turning motion can therefore end in the same discrete heading bin as a straight motion. When the corresponding position bins also coincide, the two physically different poses compete for a single stored representative.

This occurred directly at the parallel-parking goal pose (-1.35, 3.75, 0). After a 0.3 m forward step, the straight primitive and all five collision-free positive-curvature primitives mapped to the same key, (41, 69, 0). Straight motion was generated first. Because the primitives had equal length and equal cost, the later turning candidates were pruned. The maximum-curvature candidate would have reached approximately (-1.050288, 3.761387, 4.3516°), but its different orientation did not survive the merging rule. The recorded primitive outcomes contain the poses, keys and retention decisions.

The working hypothesis is that single-representative merging removes poses needed to negotiate the narrow slot. Small differences at the rear-axle reference point can produce much larger differences at the front corners of a long vehicle, so poses sharing a grid key need not admit the same collision-free continuation. Wider slots may tolerate the retained representative, while the narrow slot requires a more specific sequence of orientations and forward/reverse movements. The observed pruning is direct evidence; the relative contributions of heuristic order, position-bin alignment and tie-breaking have not been isolated for every failure. Hybrid A* already associates continuous poses with discrete cells in its original formulation; the present finding concerns the consequences of this particular implementation and parameter combination. See Dolgov et al..

A second hypothesis concerns search direction. Starting at the parked pose makes the planner negotiate the restricted slot-exit manoeuvre first, before connecting to an initial pose in the more open aisle. Because the current geometric model permits both forward and reverse travel, that solution can be traversed in the opposite order to obtain a parking path. This may change which useful poses survive merging and when an analytic connection becomes available. It does not make the algorithm complete, and coarse heading bins can still block the goal-side search almost immediately. Direction and resolution therefore need to be tested together.

The investigation also separated the searched curve from its exported representation. Search primitives and Reeds–Shepp connections are piecewise constant-curvature motions. The existing export stage interpolates position and heading at 0.3 m intervals along the accumulated path, and the subsequent check interpolates between those exported poses. An output interval can span a curvature switch or a direction reversal. The interpolated motion then differs from the curve that the search actually checked. In a path close to the 5 cm clearance threshold, even a small representation error can change the acceptance result. This suggests an interface defect that should be investigated independently of search failure or safety-margin selection.

3. Validation design

The first experiment used the ten initial poses of the narrowest parallel layout in a two-factor comparison: start-to-goal versus goal-to-start search, each with 5° or 2.5° heading bins. Position bins remained at 0.2 m. Vehicle dimensions, minimum turning radius, obstacle geometry, the 0.3 m primitive length, eleven curvature choices, heuristic inflation, motion costs and the 5 cm clearance requirement were held fixed. No search had a time limit; the 200,000-expansion cap remained. The forty case-setting records comprise ten verified historical forward-baseline records and thirty fresh searches, with the reverse-5° arm recorded as a supplementary comparison.

The successful combination—goal-side search with 2.5° heading bins—was then applied in 24 fresh, independent searches to the entire frozen failure cohort. Reusing the same setting across the other scene families tests whether the observed recovery extends beyond the narrow parallel layout. It is an extension within the development benchmark, not an independent test of generalization to unseen environments.

For each returned solution, the actual primitive and Reeds–Shepp segments were recorded. To restore the original task direction, the segment order and signed travel lengths were reversed while curvature and the vehicle-heading convention were preserved. No additional 180° rotation was applied to the body heading. This operation is valid for the reversible geometric model used here; it does not supply a physically feasible velocity profile or account for steering-actuator dynamics. The relevant bounded-curvature geometry is described by Reeds and Shepp.

Acceptance used an independent interval check on the reconstructed arcs, including the complete vehicle body, obstacle clearance, map boundaries, curvature and endpoint consistency. For each interval, a midpoint clearance is reduced by an upper bound on body motion and a numerical allowance. If the resulting lower bound is positive, the interval is accepted; otherwise it is subdivided or marked as violating or unresolved. Subdivision depth and query count are capped at 12 and 16,000. An unresolved interval is not accepted. This is a conditional numerical verification under the stated geometric and floating-point assumptions, rather than a dense-sampling-only test or a physical safety certification.

The primary recovery protocol also required the original exported-pose check to pass. Keeping that requirement made representation failures visible instead of silently changing the success criterion. A separate follow-up experiment addressed only candidates whose original arcs passed but whose exports failed. It regenerated poses directly from the same arcs at intervals no longer than 0.05 m, preserving every primitive boundary, and repeated the existing export check. Neither the search nor the underlying curve was changed, and the primary results were retained separately.

4. Results and interpretation

The narrow parallel comparison showed a strong interaction between direction and heading resolution:

Initial pose to parking goal1/10 (10%)1/10 (10%)

Finer heading bins alone did not improve the aggregate forward result. They recovered one previously failed case but lost the previously successful one. Goal-side search alone was also insufficient: with 5° bins, all ten runs exhausted their queues after fifteen expansions. The combined setting recovered all ten cases. This supports the proposed interaction between pose merging and search direction; it does not support a rule that finer discretization always improves success. The nine originally missing paths now have independently searched and verified geometric solutions, strengthening the earlier evidence from constructed feasibility witnesses.

Across the full 24-case failure cohort, 22 searches returned paths and all 22 original arc sequences passed the interval checks. Seventeen also passed the original export check. The other five passed after the separate arc-preserving export correction:

The five export failures were clearance violations, not observed body overlap at the reported samples. First violating samples had obstacle distances of approximately 0.04998708 m or 0.04960126 m. These values are close to 0.05 m but remain below the declared threshold. Passing the same check after exporting from the unchanged arcs supports representation inconsistency as the cause for these five candidates. It provides no reason to widen the acceptance tolerance. Nor does successful sampled export, by itself, prove that every interpolated output interval is safe; the continuous geometric evidence belongs to the recorded original arcs.

Fourteen of the sixteen original no-path cases and all eight original export-failure cases were recovered. Excluding the nine parallel failures used during method selection leaves 13 recoveries among 15 additional failure cases. The two unresolved cases are irregular_concave_notch_x8_y8.5 and tpcap_case07_project_4.7x2, whose goal-side searches exhausted the queue after 95,841 and four expansions, respectively. The latter previously reached the 200,000-expansion cap in unlimited-time forward search. No feasible witness has been established for these two cases, so their physical feasibility remains undetermined.

Relative to the unlimited-time forward baseline, the primary protocol adds thirteen accepted cases, and the export correction adds another five. Retaining historical forward successes gives a combined sampled-acceptance ledger of 121/128, or 126/128 after the export correction. The four TPCAP cases already recovered by removing the time limit are not counted twice. The 126/128 figure is not a full-corpus continuous-verification rate: historical successful paths lack the complete recorded arc sequences used for the new interval checks. It is also not an H-OBCA or end-to-end parking success rate.

5. Proposed resolution and remaining work

The evidence supports retaining the original forward search and adding goal-side search with 2.5° heading bins as a fallback when the forward attempt stops without an acceptable path. This preserves existing successes and avoids the regression observed when heading refinement replaces the forward configuration globally. Each attempt should keep its own stop reason, candidate-validation outcome and expansion count. A queue exhausted, an expansion cap reached and a program error require different diagnoses; none should be reported as proof that a continuous solution does not exist.

The export interface should retain the original geometric segments as the authoritative path representation. Sampled poses should be generated from those segments, with curvature switches and changes of travel direction preserved. A consumer that interpolates or resamples the output creates a representation that may require its own validation. Passing the original arc check cannot automatically certify a different curve reconstructed later. The five-case export experiment establishes a practical correction for the observed failures, while broader regression testing is still needed before applying it to every path.

For the two unresolved cases, the next diagnosis should examine the first feasible successors, key collisions and retained poses near both endpoints. Controlled comparisons can then test shorter primitives, several distinct representatives per key, or a bidirectional search. These are candidate remedies, not validated results in the present study. Changing them one at a time would help determine whether a failure reflects local pruning, inadequate motion choices or the expansion cap. Increasing wall-clock time is relevant only when time is actually the stopping condition. Existing work on multi-heuristic parking search and bidirectional Hybrid A* provides alternatives; the sequential fallback evaluated here is not a new bidirectional-search formulation.

Recovered paths must also be checked at the interface to trajectory optimization. The narrow parallel solutions can contain multiple direction reversals; geometric feasibility does not establish feasibility within the current fixed physical horizon, acceleration limits or steering-rate limits. The same recovered initial paths should therefore be supplied to the node-only, fixed-additional-point and adaptive-additional-point optimizers, with front-end availability, conditional optimizer acceptance and end-to-end acceptance reported separately. No such backend rerun was performed in this recovery study.

The contribution of the investigation is an evidence-based separation of search loss and representation loss, supported by fixed inputs, direct pruning records, controlled comparisons and independent geometric checks. It demonstrates recovery on the selected failures without reducing the turning radius or clearance requirement. Because method selection used part of the failure cohort and several cases share layouts, the results describe a development benchmark; an independently frozen set of new layouts is needed to support broader claims.
